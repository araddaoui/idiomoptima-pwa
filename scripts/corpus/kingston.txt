In the context of indigenous histories, peoples, languages and/or cultures and their representations in literature, what could link Maxine Hong Kingston and Fatima Mernissi? I assume their similar representations of the indigenous Hawaiians and Amazighs. Such a common denominator has the asset of allowing me to address fictional and nonfictional embodiments of indigenous histories, peoples, languages and/or cultures while remaining within the continuity of Maxine Hong Kingston’s aesthetic and political legacy in its sensitivity to issues of gender and armed resistance versus political activism.1 In certain ways, Kingston’s writings strongly bring to mind Fatima Mernissi’s memoir published in 1994 and entitled Dreams of Trespass: Tales of a Harem Girlhood. Both writers have affinities with each other at several levels, among them the fact that Mernissi organizes her memoir around gender and/or political activism, both of which provide inseparable cornerstones in Kingston’s literary corpus, extending from The Woman Warrior (1976) to China Men (1977), To Be the Poet (2002), The Fifth Book of Peace (2005) and her book-length and recent collection of poetry, I Love a Broad Margin to My Life (2011). The argument that Dreams of Trespass relatively moves along a Kingstonian tradition lends itself basically to the mere fact that Mernissi writes within the parameters of gender and resistance even through her underpinning representation of Amazigh people and their cultural residuals in colonized Morocco.
